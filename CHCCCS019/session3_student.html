Crisis Intervention Principles

Stages of Crisis Intervention

People experiencing crisis move through stages — your response needs to adapt at each stage.

  • 1
    Recognise that there is a crisis.
  • 2
    Identify the significance of the triggering event and its impact on your client.
  • 3
    Release tension by listening and providing emotional support.
  • 4
    Encourage realism — help the person avoid catastrophising by focusing on the realities of the situation (cognitive restructuring).
  • 5
    Make plans — prioritise needs and issues, set achievable goals.
  • 6
    Build on strengths — empower the client and offer alternatives. Allow them to feel in control as much as possible.

Intervention Models

Two Key Frameworks

Psychological First Aid (PFA)

  1. Listen — don't pressure people to talk
  2. Provide comfort; help people feel calm
  3. Connect people to information, services and social supports
  4. Protect people from further harm

Roberts' Seven Stage Model

  1. Assess lethality and mental health status
  2. Establish rapport and engage the client
  3. Identify major problems
  4. Deal with feelings
  5. Explore alternative coping and partial solutions
  6. Develop an action plan
  7. Develop a termination and follow-up protocol
Critical Incidents

What is a Critical Incident?

A critical incident is any event that poses a serious risk to the life, health or safety of a person receiving services. This includes assault, threats, severe injury, death, fire, bomb threats, self-harm, violent behaviour, missing clients, medication errors, and suicide or attempted suicide.

Critical Incident Stress Management (CISM)

1. Prepare ▼

Provide education and training for crisis before it occurs. Prevention and preparation are essential.

2 & 3. Defuse (immediately after) ▼

Defusing — immediately after an incident — gives those affected a chance to ventilate and express feelings. Should happen as soon as possible, ideally within 3 days and at the site of the incident.

4. Debrief (within a week) ▼

Critical Incident Stress Debriefing (CISD) — usually within a week — helps address acute impacts, decide on follow-up, and provide a sense of closure.

5–7. Counselling and referral ▼

Individual crisis intervention counselling (may be ongoing). Family/organisational support if required. Referral to follow-up supports and services.

Emergency Interventions & CATT

Emergency interventions include calling emergency services, 'talking down' a person intent on self-harm, protecting others, removing people from danger, and (last resort) physical or chemical restraints.

The Crisis Assessment and Treatment Team (CATT) responds 24/7 to mental health crises including psychosis, self-harm, suicidality, and feeling out of control.

Signs & Referrals

Identifying Safety Issues and Making Referrals

Direct signs

Observable indicators: visible physical injury (bruises, wounds), threatening behaviour, visible fear, overt aggression or violence.

Indirect signs

Verbal/nonverbal 'hints': fear contradicted by words, inappropriate laughter, flinching, cowering, avoiding a particular person, threatening gestures, invading personal space.

🔗
Referrals: Discuss options with client → obtain consent → provide info → follow org procedures → check eligibility → provide info to receiving agency → follow up to check outcomes.
