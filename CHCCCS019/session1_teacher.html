Teacher Scripts

Word-for-Word Facilitation Notes

⚠️ Trigger Warning — say this at the start

This unit deals with sensitive topics including suicide, self-harm, domestic violence, and child abuse. We'll be discussing these professionally and respectfully. If anything we cover brings up difficult feelings for you, you are welcome to step out — no questions asked. Box Hill TAFE has counselling services available. Look after yourselves and each other.

Opening — What is a crisis? (Slide 7)

"Before we define a crisis professionally, I want you to think about a time you've experienced one yourself — or watched someone go through it. Not necessarily a major disaster — it might have been something that felt overwhelming in the moment. A relationship ending. A job loss. An exam result. Failing to pay rent. What made it a crisis wasn't the size of the event — it was that the person couldn't cope the way they usually would. That's the key. (pause — invite brief sharing) In community services, our working definition is: a crisis is a state in which a person is unable to function in their usual way due to a significantly stressful event."

Suicide — framing this topic (Slide 16–17)

"We're now going to talk about potential suicide. I know this might feel confronting, but as community services workers, this is something you will encounter. Research tells us that asking someone directly about suicide does NOT increase the risk — it actually decreases it, because it tells the person someone is willing to have the conversation. The three principles are simple: Ask. Listen and stay. Get help. Let me walk through each of these."

DFV — distinguishing the terms (Slide 24–25)

"There's an important distinction here between domestic violence and family violence. Domestic violence specifically refers to behaviour between current or former intimate partners — one person using power and control over the other, usually through fear. Family violence is broader — it covers violence between any family members, and it's the preferred term when working with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities because it acknowledges extended family and kinship structures. In practice you'll use both terms — know the difference."

Safety planning wrap-up (after SMART goals)

"The key word here is 'collaborative.' We're not making safety plans for people — we're making them with them. Imposing a plan on someone in crisis takes away their agency and often doesn't work. At some point you might need to be directive — if someone is in immediate danger, you act. But your default is to keep the person at the centre of decision-making as much as their capacity allows. That balance — between collaboration and direction — is something we'll come back to in Session 4."

Discussion Prompts

Class Discussion Questions

Discussion prompts — use as needed
  • What's the difference between a crisis and just a really bad day? Where's the line?
  • Can you think of a news event from the past year that triggered a crisis for many people? What kinds of support were needed?
  • Why might indirect indicators of crisis be harder to spot than direct ones? What makes them easy to miss?
  • In the John and Mary case study — who is in crisis? Is Mary also at risk? What would you do as a support worker?
  • Why do you think directly asking someone about suicide actually reduces risk rather than increasing it?
  • What do you think the hardest part of working with someone in a DFV situation would be? Why?
Quiz Answers

Student Quiz — Benchmark Answers

Q1B — A state in which a person is unable to function in their usual way, often due to a significantly stressful event. Not all crises involve disasters; the key is the person's inability to cope using their usual resources.
Q2C — Withdrawal and increased alcohol use are indirect indicators — behavioural changes that suggest distress without directly referencing the crisis. Options A, B and D are direct indicators.
Q3C — Ask directly: 'Are you thinking about suicide?' Research shows direct questioning reduces risk. Never wait for the person to bring it up — asking shows you care and opens the conversation.
Q4B — Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Timely. Safety planning goals should meet all five criteria to be effective.
Differentiation

Supporting Your Cohort

Support strategies
  • Pre-teach key vocabulary before the session (crisis, indicator, mandatory reporting)
  • Provide the Key Model Handout as a reference throughout activities
  • Allow verbal responses to activity questions instead of written
  • Pair EAL/D learners with a supportive peer for group activities
  • Offer sentence starters for reflection tasks
  • Allow extra time for AT1 where reasonable adjustment applies
Extension strategies
  • Research Gerard Caplan's crisis theory and report back to the group
  • Investigate one Victorian piece of legislation relevant to the crisis types covered today
  • Find a current news story involving a crisis situation — apply the indicators framework
  • Compare the 'Ask, Listen and Stay, Get Help' model with the Lifeline resources

EAL/D note: Several students may find the DFV and suicide content particularly confronting or culturally sensitive. Check in privately during break or lunch if you notice any student becoming withdrawn or distressed.

Disclosure protocol: This content may trigger disclosures. If a student discloses personal experience of DFV, abuse or suicidal ideation, respond calmly, thank them for sharing, and follow BHI's student welfare and mandatory reporting procedures. Do not probe further in class.
