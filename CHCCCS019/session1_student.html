Key Concepts

Defining a Crisis

What is a crisis?

A turning point — a time of great difficulty, danger, or important change. In community services, it means a person is unable to function in their usual way due to an overwhelming event.

Crisis intervention

Short-term, immediate help that addresses the crisis, reduces distress, and supports the person to identify resources and regain their ability to function.

Triggering events

Situations (natural disasters, violence, financial hardship), relationships (breakdown, abuse), or life changes (adolescence, ageing, loss of employment).

Direct indicators

Observable signs: a triggering event has occurred, distorted perceptions, inability to solve problems, overwhelming emotions stopping the person from functioning.

Indirect indicators

Less obvious signs: changes in social engagement, mood, physical health, skills, or use of drugs and alcohol — behavioural 'red flags.'

Communication in crisis

People revert to fight-or-flight thinking. They miss information, fill gaps with speculation, and may feel fear, dread, or denial. Their responses can be exaggerated or distorted.

Flip Cards — Types of Crisis

Know Your Crisis Types

Click each card to reveal how to respond.

Potential Suicide tap to reveal

Ask directly. Listen and stay — do not leave them alone. Get help — call Lifeline 13 11 14 or 000 if life is in danger.

Self-Harm tap to reveal

Provide first aid if needed. Use active listening. Remain calm and non-judgmental. Encourage professional help from someone they trust.

Threats of Harm tap to reveal

Identify who is at risk. If imminent — remove yourself and others, then call for help. Follow org policies and procedures.

Received Threats tap to reveal

Keep everyone safe. Follow your org's policies and procedures. Use common sense. Err on the side of caution — notify police if the situation warrants.

Abuse tap to reveal

Protect safety and wellbeing. Follow legal requirements (child protection legislation). Use rights-based, person-centred, strengths-based approaches.

Domestic & Family Violence tap to reveal

DFV = power and control through fear. Family violence is broader, covering extended family/kinship. Prioritise safety of the person experiencing violence.

Safety Planning

Addressing Safety Issues

Safety is always your primary concern when responding to crisis. These steps may not always happen in this order.

  • 1
    Listen empathetically — collect information about the situation.
  • 2
    Affirm the person's links to safety and living.
  • 3
    Support the person to identify possible solutions and strategies.
  • 4
    Balance collaborative and directive approaches — adapt to the person's capacity to make decisions.
  • 5
    Agree on actions to minimise risks.
  • 6
    Call for emergency assistance if required.
  • 7
    Check your actions are legal, ethical, and consistent with your org's duty of care policies.
💡
SMART goals for safety planning: Specific · Measurable · Achievable · Relevant · Timely
