The Scenario

Read this carefully before the role play begins. You will be given a copy on the day.

📋 Your Scenario — Team Conflict at First Access

You work at First Access as a Support Worker. Two male casual Support Workers don't appear to get along very well. One is Indigenous, named Ryan, and the other is Muslim, named Haider, who has English as an additional language — it is sometimes hard to understand Haider when he gets overwhelmed.

One day their issue escalated and they started to yell at each other. Haider accused Ryan of being lazy and rude, and Ryan made comments about Haider leaving the clients to go and pray. Dianne, an Australian-born Support Worker, gets caught up with Ryan and Haider complaining all the time about each other and tries to get them to take sides.

Their conflict impacts the staff and the clients. In this instance, their shouts could be heard in the office where the Supervisor was in a meeting with a client.

You will need to intervene between Ryan, Haider and Dianne to diffuse the conflict in a professional manner.

Remember: You are not there to decide who is right or wrong. You are there to de-escalate, communicate respectfully, and then brief your supervisor.

You are a Support Worker at First Access. You arrive to find your colleagues in a heated argument. You don't know the full history of their conflict, but you can see it's escalating and affecting the workplace.

Ryan
Indigenous Support Worker — peer plays this role
▼

Background

Ryan is an Indigenous Support Worker. His communication style reflects aspects of Aboriginal culture — he tends not to make direct eye contact and works at his own pace. He's been frustrated with Haider's comments about his work pace and has reacted badly today.

How to play this role

  • Start by muttering to yourself about running behind — you're supervising clients packing up board games and they're moving slowly
  • When Haider comments, respond defensively: "Look who's talking. The one that leaves everything to go and pray."
  • Escalate if Haider pushes back: "Just take your stupid rug and go pray... who cares if you take extra time off."
  • When the Support Worker intervenes: gradually de-escalate if they are calm and respectful. If they take sides or seem dismissive, stay resistant.
  • Do NOT make it impossible — this is a realistic situation, not a trap.
Key lines from the scene
"Look who's talking."
"Just take your stupid rug and go pray... who cares if you take extra time off."
Ryan's indirect communication style and relationship with time are aspects of his cultural background — not laziness. A culturally competent worker recognises this difference.

Haider
Muslim Support Worker, EAL/D — peer plays this role
▼

Background

Haider is a Muslim Support Worker for whom English is an additional language. He becomes harder to understand when he's upset — he stutters and loses words. His religion requires him to pray at certain times, which has caused conflict with Ryan.

How to play this role

  • Start by commenting to Dianne about Ryan: "He never hurries, even when something urgent happens. I've had enough of him."
  • When Ryan responds, get visibly upset: "C-c-come on man, l-l-look in me eyes if y-y-you have something t-t-to tell me. What's your p-p-problem?"
  • When the Support Worker intervenes: respond to patience and calm. If they rush you or seem frustrated with your stutter, become more distressed.
  • Haider DOES need extra time and patience to communicate — this is part of what's being assessed in the student.
Key lines from the scene
"He never hurries, even when something urgent happens."
"C-c-come on man, l-l-look in me eyes if y-y-you have something t-t-to tell me. What's your p-p-problem?"
Haider's prayer practice is a religious right — not an optional extra. An employer who prevents him practising his religion risks breaching anti-discrimination law.

Dianne
Australian-born Support Worker — peer plays this role
▼

Background

Dianne is well-meaning but tends to gossip and get drawn into drama. She's not malicious — she just doesn't have good professional boundaries. Today she's fanning the flames by taking sides and commenting.

How to play this role

  • Get drawn into the argument and comment: "Enough is enough you two! Why can't you just be like the rest of us?"
  • If the Support Worker addresses you directly, be slightly defensive but ultimately cooperative — you're not the villain here.
  • You are not the main focus of the scene — your role is to show that workplace conflicts often have bystanders who unintentionally make things worse.
Key line from the scene
"Enough is enough you two! Why can't you just be like the rest of us?"
Dianne's comment "just be like the rest of us" is itself a form of cultural pressure — a culturally safe worker would recognise this even while managing the larger conflict.

Scene Breakdown — What to Expect

The scene has three phases. Knowing what's coming will help you stay confident.

Scene 1
The conflict escalates

It is 10:00am. Ryan is supervising clients packing up board games. They are running behind and morning tea is already out. Haider comments to Dianne about Ryan. Ryan overhears. The argument escalates. Dianne jumps in and makes it worse.

This is already happening when you walk in — you don't start the scene, you enter it.

↓
Scene 2
You intervene

You step in as the Support Worker. There is no script — your job is to respond naturally and professionally.

  • Stay calm and professional throughout
  • Communicate respectfully with all three colleagues
  • Not take sides or make value judgements
  • Give Haider extra time and patience — he stutters when upset
  • Be aware of Ryan's communication style — less eye contact is cultural, not dismissive
  • Make sure everyone feels heard and included

The scene ends when the conflict is de-escalated to a reasonable point.

↓
Scene 3
Supervisor debrief

After the conflict is de-escalated, approach your teacher (playing the Supervisor) and brief them on what happened. In your debrief you should:

  • Describe the situation clearly and without taking sides
  • Acknowledge the value of both Ryan and Haider to the team
  • Mention that preventing Haider from practising his religion could breach anti-discrimination law
  • Suggest at least one strategy to improve cultural safety in the team going forward

Assessment Criteria — AT3A

Your assessor will be watching for all 6 of these. You need all 6 to achieve Satisfactory.

1
Respectful communication with diverse colleagues
You are patient and calm throughout. You don't rush anyone. You don't make negative facial expressions or dismissive gestures. You allow people time to re-group — especially Haider.
Linked to PC 3.1
2
Professional approach — no taking sides
You don't make judgements about who is right or wrong. You don't join in with Dianne's side-taking. You treat all three colleagues with equal respect regardless of your personal opinion of the situation.
Linked to PC 3.2
3
Inclusive work practices
Everyone is involved in the conversation. You consider individual needs — including cultural ones. You make it clear through your actions that each person's background and values matter.
Linked to PC 4.1
4
Effective verbal and non-verbal communication
Appropriate eye contact — aware that Ryan's culture involves less direct eye contact. Calm tone of voice. Awareness of personal space. No pointing. Your body language matches your words.
Linked to PC 3.2
5
Sensitive handling of language barriers
You actively listen when Haider speaks. You don't raise your voice or show frustration when he stutters. You may repeat information back to check understanding. You do not speak over him or finish his sentences.
Linked to PC 3.3
6
Seeks supervisor assistance and debriefs
You approach the Supervisor (teacher) at the end. You describe the situation without taking sides. You mention the value of both Ryan and Haider. You raise the anti-discrimination issue re: Haider's religion. You suggest at least one forward strategy.
Linked to PC 3.4
There are no exact right words. Your assessor is watching your approach and your professional judgement — not a performance. A calm, respectful, inclusive approach is what earns a Satisfactory result.

Tips for Your Role Play

Six things to keep in mind when the scene gets intense.

💬
Start by listening

Don't rush in with solutions. Take a moment to observe and understand what's actually happening before you speak. People feel respected when they feel heard first.

🧘
Calm is contagious

Your tone sets the tone for the room. If you stay calm and speak slowly, the people around you will usually start to match your energy. Don't match the aggression — lower it.

⚖️
Don't pick a side

Even if one person seems more 'right' than the other, taking sides will make things worse. Acknowledge both perspectives: "I can hear that this is really frustrating for both of you."

🗣️
Give Haider time

When Haider is upset, he stutters and struggles to find words. Do not rush him, finish his sentences, or speak over him. Slow down, make eye contact, and wait. This is one of the key things your assessor is watching for.

🏢
Remember the workplace context

You are a Support Worker in a community services setting. Clients may be nearby. Your professional behaviour here affects not just your colleagues, but the people you all serve.

👔
End with the supervisor

Don't forget this step. Walking to your teacher for a debrief is one of the six assessment criteria. When the conflict feels de-escalated, that's your cue to approach the supervisor.
