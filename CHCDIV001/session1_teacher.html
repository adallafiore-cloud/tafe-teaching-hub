👋 Welcome & Opening Prompt
FacilitationWelcome — what to say and do
▾

Before students arrive: Write on the whiteboard — "What comes to mind when you hear the word 'diversity'?" Do NOT add your own answer.

"Welcome everyone. My name is [name]. Before we get into anything, I want to know what's already in the room. Take 30 seconds — what comes to mind when you see that word on the board?"

Take responses without commenting. Write them all. You will return to this list at 2:45pm to see if thinking has shifted.

"Then go around the room — name + one word that describes your background. Could be cultural, professional, geographic, generational — whatever feels right."
This room already contains real diversity. Name it: "This room already contains more diversity than most workplaces in Australia. That's a genuine strength for this unit."
For students who seem hesitant about sharing — it's fine to say "pass" or just give their name. Do not pressure.

9:45 – 10:05 (20 min)
🔑 Three Key Concepts

This is the highest-value content for assessments. Go slowly. Students need all three for AT1 Q1.

Concept 1
🧠 Cultural Awareness
Understanding your own cultural background and how it differs from others. Knowing that everyone has different values, beliefs, and ways of seeing the world.
Example: A worker who grew up in a very punctual household learns that in some cultures, time works differently — and adjusts their expectations.
Concept 2
💼 Cultural Competence
The ability to work effectively and ethically with people from different backgrounds — applies to both individuals AND organisations.
Example: A community org trains all staff in diverse communication styles, and ensures its policies include CALD clients.
Concept 3
🛡️ Cultural Safety
Creating an environment where people are not exposed to challenge, rejection, or harm because of who they are. About dignity, respect, and genuine listening.
Example: A worker uses a client's correct pronouns without being asked, because they asked respectfully what they prefer.
Facilitation + DiscussionKey Concepts — how to teach & discussion prompt
▸
"Which of these three do you think is hardest to achieve in a community services workplace? Why?"

Take 2–3 responses. There's no single right answer — encourage debate. Safety tends to be the most challenging because it requires systemic change, not just individual awareness.

"The journey goes: awareness first, then competence, then safety. You can't have safety without the others underneath it."
Use the continuum metaphor: draw a simple arrow on the whiteboard — Awareness → Competence → Safety. Students find this visual memorable for assessments.
After explaining, invite students to write a one-sentence definition in their own words before moving on. This helps LLN learners encode the vocabulary.

FacilitationIceberg — what to say + discussion prompt
▸
"Most of what shapes how we see the world is invisible — including to ourselves. Cultural misunderstandings happen not because people are rude, but because the invisible parts of their icebergs are different."

Brief share prompt (2–3 people only — keep it brief):

"Has anyone had a miscommunication with someone from a different background? What happened? What was the invisible bit that caused the misunderstanding?"
The below-the-waterline items are the most important for community services work. Emphasise: "This is what we navigate every time we start with a new client, colleague, or family."
Use a real, simple example — e.g. eye contact: in Australian mainstream culture, eye contact shows respect. In many other cultures, avoiding eye contact shows respect. Neither is wrong.

Social Identity Wheel

📋 AT Connection

This activity is direct source material for AT4 Part A Q1 and Q2. Tell students to keep their worksheets.

Distribute the Social Identity Wheel worksheets (or direct students to the Student HTML).

Full InstructionsSocial Identity Wheel — step by step
▾

Step 1 — Individual fill-in (10 min): Students fill in the wheel with their identity markers — cultural background, where they grew up, generation, family role, work experience, why they chose community services, etc.

Step 2 — Written reflection (10 min): Students answer:

  • Which identities do you think about most often? Why?
  • Which do you rarely think about — why?
  • Which have shaped how you approach helping others?

Step 3 — Pair share (10 min): Share ONE thing from the wheel with a partner — only what they're comfortable sharing.

Step 4 — Whole group debrief (10 min):

  • "What did this exercise bring up for you?"
  • "Any surprises — something you hadn't thought about before?"
  • "This reflection is exactly what AT4 Part A asks you to write about. You've started it today."

"Model briefly — share one item from your own wheel first. Something that shaped your path into education or community services. This makes it safe for others."
Experienced workers: ask how their professional identity has shifted over time. Newer students: personal reflection IS a professional skill — reframe the activity as a workplace skill, not just personal navel-gazing.
CALD students: their cultural knowledge is a genuine academic contribution. For LLN learners: the wheel format (fill in boxes) is far more accessible than blank-page writing. The Student HTML has it in digital fill-in format too.

New Learning 2
Unconscious Bias
20 minutes

Key message: Having bias is not a moral failure — it is how the brain works. What matters is being willing to notice it.

TypeIn community services practice
Affinity biasEngaging more with clients who share your communication style or background
Confirmation biasLooking for information that confirms what you already think about a person or family
Attribution biasExplaining your own mistakes differently from how you explain a client's mistakes
Halo / horns effectOne positive or negative trait colours your entire view of a person
Generational biasAssumptions based on age — in all directions (including older workers about younger clients)

FacilitationBias Audit — setup and close
▾
"This one is private. You don't need to share a word of it. But it takes honesty. Everyone has biases — the research is clear on this. This is not about being a good or bad person. It's about building the self-awareness that this unit — and this whole profession — requires."

Energy Re-Starter
Group Iceberg Mapping
30 minutes
Full InstructionsGroup Iceberg — purpose, setup, facilitation
▾

Purpose: Energy re-starter after lunch, reinforces the iceberg model in an applied context, activates the diversity in the room.

Setup: Small groups of 3–4 people — deliberately mix ages and experience levels. Give each group a large piece of paper and markers.

Instructions to read aloud:

"Draw an iceberg. Above the waterline: things you'd notice about each other within the first 5 minutes of meeting. Below the waterline: things that took time to learn, or might never be visible. You have 12 minutes."

Debrief (8 min): Each group shares ONE item from below the waterline.

"This is exactly what we navigate every time we start with a new client, colleague, or family. Their iceberg is mostly invisible — and so is ours."
Move around the room — give energy to quiet groups. Early finishers: "What assumptions might someone make about your group just from what's above the line?"
The variety in the room (ages, backgrounds, experience levels) IS the learning. Call it out positively: "Look at how much depth is in this room."
