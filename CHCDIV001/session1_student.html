🌈 What is Diversity?

In community services, diversity means recognising that every person is different — and that's a strength, not a problem.

These are the 9 areas of diversity you need to know for your assessments:

♿
Disability
Physical, sensory, intellectual, mental health, acquired brain injury
🌏
Culture
Customs, social norms, family practices, shared beliefs
👥
Race & Ethnicity
Shared language, religion, customs, place of origin
🙏
Religion & Spirituality
Beliefs, practices, ceremonies — not the same thing
⚧
Gender & Transgender
Gender identity — not the same as biological sex
🏳️‍🌈
Sexual Orientation
Lesbian, gay, bisexual, heterosexual, and more
⚧️
Intersex
Born with sex characteristics not exclusively male or female
🎂
Generational
Gen Z, Millennials, Gen X, Boomers — different values and experiences

Part 2 — AT1 Key Content
🔑 Three Key Concepts

These three concepts are essential for working in community services. You will need to define all three for Assessment Task 1 Question 1.

Concept 1
🧠 Cultural Awareness
Understanding your own cultural background and how it differs from others. Knowing that everyone has different values, beliefs, and ways of seeing the world.
Example: A worker who grew up in a very punctual household learns that in some cultures, time works differently — and adjusts their expectations.
Concept 2
💼 Cultural Competence
The ability to work effectively and ethically with people from different backgrounds. This applies to individual workers AND to the organisations they work in.
Example: A community services org trains all staff in diverse communication styles, and ensures its policies include CALD clients.
Concept 3
🛡️ Cultural Safety
Creating an environment where people are not exposed to challenge, rejection, or harm because of who they are. It's about dignity, respect, and genuine listening.
Example: A worker uses a client's correct pronouns without being asked, because they have asked respectfully what they prefer.

Part 3
🧊 The Cultural Iceberg

Culture is like an iceberg. You can only see a small part of it. Most of it is hidden below the surface.

This is why cultural misunderstandings happen — not because people are rude, but because the invisible parts of their cultural icebergs are different.

👀 VISIBLE — above the waterline (easy to see)
Food Language Clothing Celebrations Music & Art Greetings
〰〰〰 WATERLINE 〰〰〰
🔍 INVISIBLE — below the waterline (takes time to see)
Values & beliefs Attitudes to authority Gender roles Family obligations Concept of time How conflict is handled Communication style Religious beliefs Notions of fairness Attitudes to eye contact
GROUP ACTIVITY
Group Iceberg Mapping 🧊
Small groups — after lunch (12:45pm)
▸

In your group (3–4 people), draw an iceberg on a large piece of paper.

  1. Write in the above-waterline section: things you could tell about each other in the first 5 minutes of meeting.
  2. Write in the below-waterline section: things that took longer to learn, or that might never be visible to a stranger.
  3. Share one item from below the waterline with the whole class.

What assumptions might someone make about your group just from what they can see?

What would they miss?

Part 5
🔎 Unconscious Bias

Bias = when we favour or disfavour something based on our background, culture, or experience.

Unconscious = we are not aware it is happening.

💡 Key idea

Having bias does not make you a bad person. It is part of how the human brain works — we all pattern-match and categorise to make sense of the world.

What matters is being willing to notice it.

These are the most common types of unconscious bias in community services:

Type of BiasWhat it looks like in practice
Affinity biasEngaging more with clients who share your background or communication style
Confirmation biasLooking for information that confirms what you already think about a person
Attribution biasExplaining your own mistakes differently from how you explain a client's mistakes
Halo / horns effectOne positive or negative trait colours your entire view of a person
Generational biasAssumptions based on age — in all directions

Reference
📖 Key Terms
Cultural Awareness
Knowing your own cultural background and understanding that others have different values, beliefs and ways of living.
You become aware that your idea of 'normal' family structure is not universal.
Cultural Competence
The ability to work effectively and respectfully with people from different cultural backgrounds.
You adjust your communication style when working with a CALD (Culturally and Linguistically Diverse) client.
Cultural Safety
An environment where people are not exposed to challenge, rejection, or harm because of their identity.
A client feels comfortable disclosing their religion because the worker has never made dismissive comments about it.
Cultural Iceberg
A model showing that only a small part of culture is visible. The large invisible layer includes values, beliefs, and assumptions.
Unconscious Bias
A preference for or against something that you are not aware of — shaped by your background and experiences.
Unconsciously spending less time with clients who don't make eye contact, because eye contact feels 'normal' to you.
CALD
Culturally and Linguistically Diverse — a term used in Australian community services for people from non-English-speaking backgrounds.
