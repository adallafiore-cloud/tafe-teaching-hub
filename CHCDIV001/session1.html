Key Concepts

✓

By the end of today you will be able to explain these six concepts. Click each card to flip it and test yourself.

🌍 Culture

The shared customs, values, beliefs and practices of a group of people. Culture shapes how we see the world — often without us realising it.

🤝 Diversity

Differences across disability, culture, race, religion, gender, age, sexual orientation and more. In community services, understanding diversity is the work.

🧊 Cultural Iceberg

Culture has a visible surface (food, language, clothing) and a much larger invisible layer (values, beliefs, assumptions). Most of what shapes us sits below the waterline.

👁 Cultural Awareness

Understanding your own cultural background and how it shapes the way you see others. The starting point for working effectively across difference.

⚖️ Cultural Competence

The ability to work ethically and effectively in intercultural settings — both as an individual and within an organisation.

🔍 Unconscious Bias

Patterns of thought that lead us to favour or disfavour others without realising it. Not a character flaw — a feature of how every human brain works.

New Learning 1: Culture, Diversity & Key Concepts

✓

What Is Diversity?

The Australian Human Rights Commission recognises diversity across many areas of human life. As a community services worker, you will work with people from all of these groups.

Diversity AreaWhat It Covers
DisabilitySensory, intellectual, physical, mental health and acquired brain injury
CultureCustoms, social norms, family practices and shared beliefs
RaceGenetic variation and physical characteristics
EthnicityShared language, religion, customs and place of origin
Religion & SpiritualityBeliefs, practices and ceremonies — distinct from each other
Gender identityHow a person identifies internally — not the same as biological sex
IntersexBorn with sex characteristics that don't fit exclusively male or female
Sexual OrientationLesbian, gay, bisexual, heterosexual and more
GenerationalDifferent values and experiences across age groups

Three Key Concepts

Cultural Awareness

The starting point

Understanding your own cultural background and recognising how it shapes your assumptions, judgements and interactions. You can't work across difference effectively if you haven't examined your own lens first.

Example: A worker realises she assumes "professional" behaviour means direct eye contact — and that this is a cultural assumption, not a universal.

Cultural Competence

Individual + organisational

The ability to work ethically and effectively across cultural difference — as both a practitioner and an organisation. Competence includes knowledge, skills, attitudes and systemic practices.

Example: An agency revises its intake forms to include preferred pronouns and dietary requirements, and trains all staff on diverse client needs.

Cultural Safety

The gold standard

An environment where people are not exposed to challenge or rejection because of their identity. Cultural safety goes beyond awareness — it is about the experience of the client, not the intentions of the worker.

Example: A case worker uses a client's preferred name, avoids assumptions about family structure, and ensures the meeting space has no culturally offensive materials.

New Learning 2: Unconscious Bias

What Is Unconscious Bias?

  • Unconscious biases are patterns of thought that shape our judgements without us realising.
  • They are not the same as conscious prejudice — they are a feature of how every human brain works.
  • They are shaped by our upbringing, cultural background, media, lived experience and social environment.
  • They affect hiring decisions, service delivery, team dynamics and client outcomes.
  • Awareness is the first — and most important — step toward managing them.

Types of Bias

Bias TypeWhat It Looks Like
Affinity biasFavouring people who are similar to us in background, values or experience
Confirmation biasSeeking evidence that confirms what we already believe about a person or group
Attribution biasApplying different explanations to the same behaviour depending on who performs it
Halo effectOne positive trait shapes our entire view of a person
Generational biasMaking assumptions about people based on their age or generation

Warm-up: Group Iceberg Mapping

This is a physical group activity. You will need butcher's paper and markers.

  1. In groups of 3–4, draw a large iceberg on butcher's paper — make it big enough to write on.
  2. Label the waterline. The top section is visible culture; the bottom is invisible culture.
  3. Each person contributes: write 2 items above the waterline and 2 items below that reflect your own cultural background.
  4. When everyone has contributed, look at the full iceberg together. What patterns do you notice? What surprised you?
  5. Each group shares 1–2 observations with the whole class. Notice: what sits below the waterline is often where misunderstandings happen.
Teacher Note: Circulate and prompt. If a group gets stuck on below-the-waterline items, ask: "What would someone have to believe about family, time, or respect for this to make sense?" This often unlocks it. CALD students: their contributions here are textbook-level content — name that explicitly. Newer students: it's fine to write things you've only recently become aware of.
