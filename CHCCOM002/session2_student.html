Part 1 — AT1 Key Content
🏛️ Professionalism, Policies & Boundaries

Professionalism in community services is not just about being polite. It means acting with integrity within your role, following your organisation's policies, and upholding your client's dignity in every interaction.

Concept 1
Professionalism & Policies
Acting within your role, following organisational policies, and upholding client dignity. Policy provides a framework for consistent, ethical, and safe practice. It protects you, your client, and your organisation.
"Professionalism isn't just about being polite — it's about acting with integrity within your role boundaries."

Example: Following your org's communication policy when a client discloses sensitive information.
Concept 2
Person-Centred & Rights-Based
Person-centred = the client's needs, goals, and preferences drive every interaction. Rights-based = the client's rights are a non-negotiable starting point. Strengths-based = focus on what the client CAN do, not what they can't.
"These three lenses work together — you don't choose one, you apply all three."
Concept 3
Professional Boundaries
The limits that define what is appropriate within your role. They exist to protect both you and your client. Professional boundaries (set by your role and organisation) are different from personal boundaries (which protect your own wellbeing).
Example: "A client asks for your personal phone number. Your professional boundary is to provide the organisation's contact number instead."

⚡ Conflict Management

Conflict is a normal part of human interaction. In community services, how you respond to conflict defines your professionalism. Understanding the stages helps you intervene earlier.

Stages of Conflict
Latent
→
Perceived
→
Felt
→
Manifest
→
Aftermath

The earlier you identify and address conflict, the less disruptive it becomes. Don't wait for Manifest.

There are five main strategies for managing conflict. The right choice depends on the situation — but in community services, Collaborating is almost always the goal.

  • 🔕
    Avoiding When to use: the issue is trivial, or the timing is wrong. Overused? The conflict grows underground.
  • 🤝
    Accommodating When to use: preserving the relationship matters more than winning. You give in — sometimes appropriately.
  • ⚖️
    Compromising When to use: both parties give something. Good for time-limited situations. Neither side fully wins.
  • 🌟
    Collaborating — Best Option Both parties work together toward a shared solution. Takes more time but produces real resolution. Aim for this.
  • ⛔
    Competing — Rarely Appropriate One party wins, the other loses. Damages relationships. Almost never the right choice in community services.
De-Escalation — When a Client Is Distressed
  • Lower your voice — don't match their volume
  • Slow your pace — rushed responses escalate tension
  • Increase physical space — give them room
  • Use open body language — uncrossed arms, slight lean forward
  • Name the emotion: "I can see this is really frustrating"
  • Avoid 'you' statements ("You're being aggressive") — use 'I' or neutral language
📋 Dispute Resolution

Dispute resolution is different from conflict management. It involves formal processes used when informal resolution hasn't worked or when the situation requires structured intervention.

Dispute Resolution Processes
  • Negotiation — direct discussion between parties to reach agreement
  • Mediation — a neutral third party helps the parties reach resolution
  • Arbitration — a third party makes a binding decision
Know Your Obligations

In community services, you have reporting obligations when conflict involves risk to a client or worker. Know your organisation's escalation pathway — and use it. Keeping a conflict to yourself is not professionalism, it's a risk.

Session 2 — Quick Check

Question 1
A client becomes upset and starts raising their voice. Your FIRST response should be:
A) Match their energy so they feel heard
Matching high energy escalates the situation further.
B) Lower your voice and slow your pace to de-escalate
Correct — a calm, slower pace signals safety and reduces tension.
C) Tell them to calm down
Telling someone to calm down usually has the opposite effect.
D) Leave the room immediately
Leaving without warning abandons the client and may escalate things.
Question 2
The person-centred approach means:
A) The worker decides what's best for the client
This is the opposite of person-centred — it puts the worker in control.
B) The client's needs, goals, and preferences drive every interaction
Correct — the client's perspective is always the starting point.
C) The organisation's policies always come first
Policies are important but they don't override a person-centred approach.
D) The client must agree with everything you suggest
Person-centred practice respects autonomy — including the right to disagree.
Question 3
Professional boundaries exist to:
A) Keep workers emotionally detached from clients
Professional boundaries don't require detachment — empathy is still essential.
B) Protect both the worker and the client
Correct — boundaries create a safe, ethical framework for the relationship.
C) Limit the support you can give
Boundaries shape how support is given — they don't limit how much you care.
D) Follow union rules
Professional boundaries are an ethical and practice standard, not a union matter.
Question 4
Dispute resolution is different from conflict management because:
A) It involves formal processes like mediation or arbitration
Correct — dispute resolution uses structured, often third-party processes.
B) It's less serious than conflict management
Dispute resolution is typically used for more serious or unresolved situations.
C) It only applies to staff-to-staff situations
Dispute resolution can apply to client, staff, and organisational conflicts.
D) The worker resolves it independently
Dispute resolution almost always involves a third party or formal process.

Key Vocabulary
📖 Glossary

Know these terms. They appear in your assessments.

Professional boundaries
The limits that define what is appropriate within a worker's role. They protect both the worker and the client.
Example: not sharing your personal phone number with clients.
Person-centred approach
An approach where the client's own needs, goals, and preferences are the starting point for every decision and interaction.
Rights-based approach
An approach that treats client rights — to dignity, privacy, autonomy, and fair treatment — as non-negotiable.
Strengths-based approach
A practice approach that focuses on what a client CAN do and the resources they already have, rather than deficits.
Motivational interviewing
A collaborative, non-confrontational communication style that helps clients find their own motivation for change through open questions and reflective listening.
Conflict management
The process of identifying, addressing, and resolving conflict using appropriate strategies. Aims to maintain relationships and find workable solutions.
De-escalation
Techniques used to reduce the intensity of a conflict or distressed situation. Includes lowering your voice, slowing your pace, and naming the emotion.
Dispute resolution
Formal processes — such as negotiation, mediation, or arbitration — used to resolve conflict that cannot be resolved informally.
Policies and procedures
Documented organisational guidelines that direct how workers act in given situations. Policies state the 'what and why'; procedures describe the 'how'.
Assertive communication
A communication style that clearly expresses needs, boundaries, or concerns without aggression or passivity. The professional standard in community services.
