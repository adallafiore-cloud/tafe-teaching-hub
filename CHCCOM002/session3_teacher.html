New Learning 1
Meeting Types & Purpose
20 minutes
PC3.1 PC3.2 KE2.5 KE3.2
Content summary — what to teach
  • Formal meetings: AGMs, board meetings, committee meetings. Require a quorum, minutes are a legal record, specific rules of procedure may apply.
  • Informal meetings: Team check-ins, case conferences, supervision meetings. Flexible structure but still purposeful — someone is responsible for keeping things on track.
  • Online meetings: Zoom, Teams. Same principles as in-person — agenda, facilitation, minutes — but different logistics challenges.
Facilitation ScriptTeaching the content
▾
"Meetings come in different types — and knowing the type you're in shapes how you prepare and what's required of you. There are three main types you need to know for your assessment: formal, informal, and online."

Walk through the three types using the concept card on the student HTML. For each, ask: "Can anyone give me an example from community services?"

Model answers
Formal: "An AGM for a not-for-profit organisation where the annual financial report is tabled and board members are elected."
Informal: "A weekly team huddle where workers update the case manager on client progress."
Online: "A Zoom supervision session between a worker and their manager who are in different offices."
"The most important rule — for any meeting, in any format — is that it needs a clear purpose. If you can't answer the question 'what is this meeting FOR?' before it starts, it probably shouldn't be happening. This is the first thing an assessor will check in AT2 Part C."
Reference the student whiteboard list — if "clear purpose" or "agenda" appeared, point to it. "You already knew this."
Meeting norms vary across cultures. Turn-taking, use of silence, deference to hierarchy, directness — all vary significantly. None is wrong. But knowing Australian workplace meeting norms helps students navigate them with confidence. Acknowledge this explicitly.

New Learning 2
Preparing an Agenda
20 minutes
PC3.3 PC3.4 KE3.3
Facilitation ScriptTeaching the agenda
▾
"Open your student page and find the Agenda Builder. We're going to go through what a real agenda contains — and then you'll build one yourselves."

Walk through the six components on the whiteboard or screen:

  1. Meeting name and purpose
  2. Date, time, venue
  3. Attendees and apologies
  4. Agenda items with time allocations
  5. AOB (Any Other Business)
  6. Distributed in advance
"Number 6 is the one people forget. An agenda sent at the start of the meeting isn't an agenda — it's a surprise list. Send it at least 24 hours before. That's what gives everyone time to prepare."

Activity: Students open the Agenda Builder on the student HTML. Give them 8 minutes to fill in a complete agenda for a fictional scenario: "Your team needs to meet to discuss how to improve communication with clients."

Walk around while students build their agendas. Common errors to look for: no time allocations, missing apologies field, vague agenda item titles like "Discussion" with no context, forgetting AOB.
Students who struggle with written language can use dot points rather than full sentences for agenda items. What matters is the structure, not the prose.

New Learning 4
Group Dynamics — Tuckman's Model
20 minutes
PC3.5 PC3.6 KE3.2
Facilitation ScriptTeaching Tuckman's model
▾
"Welcome back. After break we're moving to group dynamics — which is the 'human' part of running a meeting. You can have a perfect agenda and still have a meeting fall apart if you don't understand what's happening in the room."
"In the 1960s, a psychologist named Bruce Tuckman described four stages that every group goes through: Forming, Storming, Norming, Performing. These aren't theoretical — you'll see them in every team you ever work in."

Walk through each stage using the visual on the student HTML. For each stage, ask the group: "Can you think of an example from a team you've been in?"

Forming
Polite, cautious. "Who are these people? What are my responsibilities?"
Storming
Conflict, testing. "I disagree with how this is being done."
Norming
Trust builds. "We've worked out how to do this together."
Performing
Effective. "We handle hard problems well as a team."
Key teaching point

Storming is NOT a sign that a team is failing. It is a normal, necessary stage. Teams that avoid conflict are often stuck in forming — polite but unproductive. A facilitator's job during storming is to hold the space, acknowledge the tension, and redirect toward the task.

"So when you're facilitating and things get tense — that's often storming. Your job is not to shut it down, but to channel it. Use open questions: 'Let's hear from someone who sees it differently.' 'What would work for everyone?' Name what's happening calmly: 'It sounds like there are some different views here — let's explore them.'"
Cultural variation in conflict: in some cultural contexts, open disagreement in a meeting is uncommon or inappropriate. Students from these backgrounds may experience storming differently — and may need specific permission to contribute in Australian workplace contexts. "In Australian workplaces, it is generally expected and valued for team members to raise concerns directly in a meeting. This is different in some cultural contexts, and neither is wrong."
Ask students: "What stage do you think THIS class is in right now?" Usually gets laughs and good discussion. Bring it back to: "Noticing the stage helps you respond — that's what this model is for."

New Learning 5
Facilitation Skills & PREP Structure
15 minutes
PC3.5 PC3.6 PC3.7
Facilitation ScriptFacilitation skills and PREP
▾
"The facilitator is not the chair — and they're not the most important person in the room. Their job is to make space for everyone else to do their best thinking. What do you think the difference is between chairing and facilitating?"

Take 2–3 responses. Key distinction: a chair manages procedure; a facilitator manages people and process.

Core facilitation skills to cover:

  • Manage time against the agenda — give warnings ("We have 5 minutes left on this item")
  • Draw in quiet voices — "We haven't heard from everyone — [name], what's your view?"
  • Manage dominant speakers — "Thanks for that — let's hear from someone who hasn't spoken yet"
  • Summarise and check: "So what I'm hearing is... Does that capture it?"
  • Keep to purpose: "That's interesting — let's come back to it under AOB. For now we need to focus on..."
  • Name and redirect conflict: "I can see there are strong views here. Let's take a moment — what's the core issue we're trying to solve?"
"For presentations and putting a view forward in a meeting, use PREP: Point, Reason, Example, Point. It keeps you focused and makes you easy to follow."

Give a quick live demonstration of PREP: "I think we should run the team meeting online. [Point] Because three of us are working from home that day. [Reason] Last month we used Zoom for the case conference and it worked well. [Example] Online is the most inclusive option for this meeting. [Point]"

Experienced workers in the room may already use facilitation skills intuitively. Prompt them: "You've probably been doing some of this without calling it facilitation — what strategies have you used in real meetings?"

New Learning 6
Recording Meeting Minutes
15 minutes
PC3.8 PC3.9 KE3.3
Facilitation ScriptTeaching meeting minutes
▾
"Last piece of theory before we practice: meeting minutes. Minutes are a legal record. In community services, they may be referred to months later — by the team, by management, or in a compliance review. They need to be accurate, objective, and written without personal opinion."

Walk through the components of minutes on the blank template:

  1. Date, time, venue
  2. Attendees (present) and apologies
  3. Confirmation of previous minutes (if applicable)
  4. Summary of discussion for each agenda item
  5. Decisions made
  6. Action items (WHO does WHAT by WHEN)
  7. Date and time of next meeting
"The action items table is the most important part of the minutes in a community services context. WHO. WHAT. BY WHEN. Every action item needs all three. Without them, nothing gets done."
Key distinction to teach

Minutes record DECISIONS and ACTIONS — not the full debate. Students often try to write down everything that was said. This produces an unreadable document. "If someone could not attend, what would they need to know? That's what goes in the minutes."

Assign the note-taker role to quieter students during the practice meeting. It is a structured, valued contribution that does not require public speaking — and it builds confidence.

Practice Activity
Run a Practice Meeting
25 minutes
PC3.1–PC3.9 PE2 PE3 PE4
Activity InstructionsPractice meeting setup and debrief
▾

Setup (5 min): Groups of 4–5. Assign or ask for a volunteer facilitator and note-taker. Remaining members are team members.

"You have 10 minutes to run a team meeting. Your agenda item is: 'What are three things our team could do to improve how we communicate with clients?' The facilitator manages the meeting. The note-taker takes minutes. Everyone else contributes."

Give groups the blank minutes template and a printed or screen-visible agenda template. Set a visible timer for 10 minutes.

Debrief (10 min): Bring the groups together. Ask the facilitators first, then open to the group:

  • "What worked in your meeting? What helped keep it on track?"
  • "What did the facilitator do well? What would they do differently?"
  • "Where did you see any Tuckman dynamics playing out?"
  • "Note-takers — was it hard to capture everything? What did you focus on?"
Watch for: facilitators who dominate rather than facilitate; note-takers who try to write verbatim rather than capture decisions; groups that skip the agenda and just have a chat. These are direct rehearsals of what to watch for in AT3 Part A.
If any student is uncomfortable being the facilitator, honour that — but encourage them to try the note-taker role. Structured roles with a job to do help anxious students participate more confidently.

Teacher observation priorities: Note which students take the facilitator role confidently — and which struggle. Groups showing storming dynamics today will need coaching before Session 5. Flag for follow-up during the 1:15 assessment work block.

Meeting Bingo
30 minutes
Activity InstructionsHow to run Meeting Bingo
▾
"Welcome back. Before we move into assessment work, we're doing Meeting Bingo to consolidate all of today's vocabulary. You have a 4x4 card with 16 terms. I'm going to read out definitions — you find the matching term and mark it. First to four in a row calls 'Bingo!'"

Bingo card terms (4x4): agenda · minutes · quorum · facilitator · apologies · AOB · action item · Tuckman · forming · storming · norming · performing · accessibility · PREP · collaborate · de-escalate

Use the interactive bingo card below — or print physical cards before class.

Click/tap terms to mark them as called. Use the definitions below to read out to students.

Definitions to read out (in any order):

  • Agenda: "A written plan for the meeting, distributed in advance, with items and time allocations."
  • Minutes: "A formal, legal record of what was discussed, decided, and who is responsible for actions."
  • Quorum: "The minimum number of people required for a meeting to be officially valid."
  • Facilitator: "The person who manages the flow of the meeting — not the most important voice, but the one who makes space for everyone."
  • Apologies: "Recorded at the start of minutes — the names of people who were invited but could not attend."
  • AOB: "Any Other Business — the standing item at the end of an agenda for matters not already listed."
  • Action item: "A specific task coming out of a meeting decision — includes WHO does WHAT by WHEN."
  • Tuckman: "The psychologist who described how groups develop through four stages."
  • Forming: "The first stage of group development — polite, cautious, roles unclear."
  • Storming: "The second stage — conflict and tension as members assert their ideas. Normal and necessary."
  • Norming: "The third stage — agreed ways of working develop, trust builds."
  • Performing: "The fourth stage — the group is highly effective and collaborative."
  • Accessibility: "Making sure a meeting is inclusive — considering timing, location, format, and language."
  • PREP: "A structure for presenting a point of view: Point, Reason, Example, Point."
  • Collaborate: "Working jointly with others — sharing ideas, responsibilities, and decisions."
  • De-escalate: "To reduce tension or conflict — calming the situation so productive discussion can continue."

Debrief (5 min): After bingo, ask: "Were there any terms you weren't sure of? Any that surprised you?" Quick whole-group clarification on the 1–2 most-uncertain terms before moving to assessment work.

AT2 Part C & AT3 Preparation
75 minutes
Teacher InstructionsWhat students work on and teacher priorities
▾
"This block is your independent assessment time. Today I want everyone focused on AT2 Part C — you need to produce a complete, professional agenda in response to the email scenario. Use the agenda builder from this morning as your template. Make sure every field is complete."

What students work on:

  • Primary focus — AT2 Part C: Students need to produce an actual agenda document, not just describe one. Check that they understand the scenario and know what decision they are responding to with the meeting.
  • Secondary focus — AT3 preparation: Students who have completed AT2 Part C can begin reviewing their AT3 Part A scenario and identifying their assigned role.

Teacher circulate priorities:

  1. Check in with students who struggled with the practice meeting — offer coaching on facilitation role and group dynamics before Session 5.
  2. Confirm AT3 groups are assigned and everyone knows their role.
  3. Support students who are unsure how to structure the AT2 Part C email response — the agenda is an attachment, the email covers who/what/why/when of the meeting.
  4. Flag any students who need additional LLN support for written components.
Students often conflate the AT2 Part C email with the agenda itself. Clarify: the email is the communication inviting people to the meeting and explaining its purpose; the agenda is the attached document that structures the meeting.

Self-Check Quiz — Answer Key

Session 3 — Answer Key

Q1: An agenda should be distributed...
Correct answer: B
In advance of the meeting
If students answer A (at the start), discuss the difference between an agenda as a preparation tool vs a surprise list. This connects directly to AT2 Part C.
Q2: The MAIN role of the meeting facilitator is to...
Correct answer: C
Manage the flow and ensure everyone contributes
Common wrong answer: D (write the minutes). Use this to reinforce the distinction between facilitator and note-taker.
Q3: Meeting minutes should be...
Correct answer: B
A factual, objective record of decisions and actions
Common confusion: A (word-for-word transcript). Clarify: minutes are a summary focused on decisions and action items — not a transcript.
Q4: A team that is arguing about everything is likely in the...
Correct answer: B
Storming stage
Reinforce: storming is normal and necessary. The danger is skipping it (staying politely stuck in forming) or never moving through it.
