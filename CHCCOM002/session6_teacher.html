Briefing — Both Scenarios
30 minutes
💬 Facilitation Script — Opening
SAY"Good morning everyone. Today is our final session for CHCCOM002 — and it is an assessment day. Before you feel any nerves, I want to say this: you have spent six sessions building exactly the skills that this assessment is testing. You are more ready than you think."
SAY"Today you'll complete AT4 — two role plays. Part A is a conflict resolution scenario. Part B is an interpreter facilitation. You are the support worker in both. I'll be playing one of the clients, and a peer will play the other."
SAY"I'm going to walk through both scenarios now so there are no surprises. Then you'll have 30 minutes of individual prep time before we start Part A."
Note: Display the student HTML page on screen. Walk through the scenario brief, the scripts, and the self-assessment checklists together. Normalise nerves — role plays feel exposing. Remind students the assessor is looking for competency, not perfection.
Part A Scenario Brief — Walk Through This With Students
Scenario — "Love at First Access"

Worker arrives at First Access reception. Two clients are in conflict:

  • Marcus — believes Diane shared his personal housing information with another client. Loud, agitated.
  • Diane — denies it completely. Defensive and upset.

Student plays the worker. Teacher and/or peer play Marcus and Diane.

💬 Script — Briefing Part A
SAY"In Part A, you'll walk into a reception area where Marcus and Diane are already in a heated argument. Your job is not to decide who's right — it's to de-escalate, make both people feel heard, and work toward a resolution. If you feel genuinely out of your depth, that's okay — the right answer is to involve a supervisor."
SAY"Take a look at your script on the student page. Those are your suggested lines — you don't have to memorise them word for word, but know the shape of the conversation."
Tip: Read through Marcus and Diane's scripts aloud so students know what escalation sounds like. Let them hear the tone, not just the words.
Part B Scenario Brief — Walk Through This With Students
Scenario — "Accessing an Interpreter"

Amara — new client. Recently arrived from Somalia. Limited English. A professional interpreter joins by phone.

Meeting purpose: explain the First Access intake process and gather Amara's support needs.

Student plays the worker. Teacher plays Amara. A peer (or teacher again) plays the interpreter.

💬 Script — Briefing Part B
SAY"Part B is quieter but just as important. You'll be conducting an intake meeting with Amara, who has recently arrived in Australia. A phone interpreter will be present. The key thing you're being assessed on is not the content of what you say — it's how you say it. Always speak TO Amara, not to the interpreter. Short sentences. Pause after each one. Check understanding regularly."
SAY"There are seven interpreter protocols listed on your student page. I'd encourage you to have that reference card in front of you during prep time."
Tip: Model good interpreter protocol briefly — demonstrate speaking to a 'client' (chair in the corner) while 'interpreter' is on the phone. Show what it looks like when done well.

AT4 Part A Role Plays — Conflict Resolution
90 minutes — students take turns as the worker
AT4 Part A
Conflict Resolution — "Love at First Access"
You play Marcus and Diane. Each student has approximately 10–15 minutes.
💬 Script — Calling Each Student In
SAY TO STUDENT"Okay [name], you're up. Before we start — take a breath. You know this. When you're ready, walk in as if you've just arrived at reception and found Marcus and Diane already arguing. I'll start the scene once you enter."
TO START SCENE[As Marcus, loudly] "She told people about my housing situation! I heard it from another client. That's MY information! I'm not coming back if this is how things work!"
Assessor note: Start with genuine escalation so the student has to de-escalate. Dial back if the student does well. If the student freezes, reduce intensity — "lower" your voice slightly to give them an entry point.
💬 Full Role Play Scripts
MARCUS[Opening] "She told people about my housing situation! I heard it from another client. That's MY information! I'm not coming back if this is how things work!"
MARCUS[If de-escalated] "...Fine. I just want to know it won't happen again. And I want an apology."
DIANE[Opening] "That is NOT true. I never said anything about Marcus. I don't even know his situation. This is unfair!"
DIANE[If given space] "...I understand he's upset. But I genuinely didn't say anything. Can we at least find out if it was actually me?"
Tips for playing Marcus: Raise voice on entry. Cross arms. Use frustration, not aggression. If student uses de-escalation well, gradually soften. If student uses "you" blame language or takes sides, escalate briefly so they have to self-correct.

Tips for playing Diane: Be defensive but not aggressive. Give openings — she wants to be believed. If the student gives her genuine space, shift to a more conciliatory tone.
💬 Script — After Each Role Play
SAY"Thank you [name]. Take a moment. I want to give you some brief feedback before you go back out."
SAY (STRENGTH FIRST)"What I noticed that worked well was... [specific observation]"
SAY (GROWTH)"One thing to think about for your practice going forward... [brief, specific]"
Keep feedback brief. This is an assessment — the student needs to know the outcome, not a full debrief right now. Save extended feedback for after all role plays are complete if time allows.
Differentiation — During Part A
  • Newer students: Reduce intensity on entry if they look overwhelmed. You can give a brief in-scene prompt as Marcus: "Look — I just want someone to listen to me."
  • Experienced workers: Give full escalation. They can handle it, and it makes the assessment more meaningful for them.
  • Quieter students: Remind them before entry — "You don't need to have all the answers. Your job is to make both people feel heard."

AT4 Part B Role Plays — Interpreter Facilitation
105 minutes — students take turns as the worker
AT4 Part B
Interpreter Facilitation — "Accessing an Interpreter"
You play Amara and (optionally) the interpreter. Each student approximately 12–15 minutes.
CALD Note — Before Starting Part B

Some students in your cohort may have lived experience with interpreter services — as clients, as family members, or as workers. Before beginning Part B, say: "Some of you may have real experience with interpreter services from the other side. That experience is genuinely valuable expertise — if you're comfortable sharing a brief observation before we begin, I'd love to hear it." Do not require this. Simply open the door.

💬 Script — Launching Part B
SAY"We're moving into Part B now — interpreter facilitation. Same process: you'll come in one at a time. I'll play Amara. [name/peer] will play the interpreter — either by phone or in person, depending on your setup. Take your time. The protocols are on the student page if you need a quick refresher before you go in."
SAY"Remember: the key things I'm watching for are whether you speak to Amara directly, whether you pause after each sentence, and whether you check understanding. Everything else flows from those three things."
💬 Full Role Play Scripts — Part B
AMARA (you)[Initial] "Yes... I am looking for... housing help. And my children school. I don't understand — what is intake?"
AMARA[After understanding is checked] "Ah, I understand now. One question — will same person help me each time?"
INTERPRETER"Amara says she is looking for housing assistance and help enrolling her children in school."
INTERPRETER"Amara says she didn't understand the word intake."
INTERPRETER"Amara asks whether she'll have consistent support."
Tips for playing Amara: Speak slowly. Use simple words and some gestures. Show warmth but also some anxiety — this is her first contact with an Australian service. If the student speaks to the interpreter instead of you, hold eye contact with the phone/interpreter to gently signal the issue.

If student makes a protocol error: Don't break scene immediately. Stay in character and let the student self-correct. Only intervene if the error persists or significantly disrupts the meeting.
💬 Script — After Each Part B Role Play
SAY"Well done, [name]. How did that feel from your end?"
SAY"What I observed was... [strength]. Something to keep building on is... [one growth point]."
Watch for these common errors: Speaking to the interpreter instead of the client. Asking compound questions (too many parts at once). Using jargon without realising — "intake", "assessment", "referral". Rushing through without pausing for interpretation. Excellent practice opportunity to name these gently.
Differentiation — During Part B
  • Newer students: If they freeze after the opening, prompt gently as Amara: "It is okay. I am happy to answer questions."
  • Experienced workers: You can add a complexity — Amara asks a question the worker isn't sure about. Watch how they handle uncertainty professionally.
  • CALD students: Affirm lived experience as expertise. If they nail the protocols, name it: "That's exactly right — and some of you may already know this from your own experience."

Teacher Perspective
📝End-of-Unit Learning Summary
What Was Taught — Teacher's Record

Session 6 completed CHCCOM002 with the final assessed role plays for AT4.

Part A (Conflict Resolution): Students demonstrated their ability to enter a live conflict, de-escalate, hold space for both parties without taking sides, identify a path forward, and recognise when to escalate to a supervisor. This tested PCs 2.1–2.5, 3.1–3.3 and core professional values around person-centred practice and rights-based approaches.

Part B (Interpreter Facilitation): Students demonstrated correct use of professional interpreters — speaking directly to the client, using short sentences with pauses, avoiding jargon, checking understanding, and maintaining cultural safety throughout. This tested PCs 3.4–3.5 and Performance Evidence requirements for working with diverse populations.

Why it matters: CALD clients are a significant portion of the community services sector. Professional interpreter use is not optional — it is a legal, ethical and quality-of-care requirement. Conflict is present in every workplace. De-escalation is a daily practice skill, not a specialist one. Both today's tasks connect directly to students' future professional identity.

Unit links: AT4 Part A draws on content from Sessions 1 (interpersonal skills), 2 (active listening), 5 (conflict and dispute resolution). AT4 Part B draws on Session 3 (communication across difference, professional boundaries) and Session 2 (person-centred approach). The complete unit has provided coherent, cumulative skill development toward these final performances.
