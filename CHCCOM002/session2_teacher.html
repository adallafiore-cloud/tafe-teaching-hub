🏛️ Professionalism, Policies & Boundaries
Facilitation ScriptOpening framing for this block
▾
"Professionalism in community services isn't just about being polite or using the right words. It's about acting with integrity — within your role, within your organisation's policies, and always in the interest of the client."

Give students a moment to look at the three concept cards on their student page, then facilitate a brief discussion on each.

"Let's start with policies. What do you think happens when a worker doesn't follow their organisation's communication policy — say, when a client discloses something sensitive?"
Expected responses / model answer
The worker may share information with the wrong people, breach confidentiality, damage trust, expose the organisation to liability, or fail to connect the client to appropriate support. Policy exists to prevent exactly these outcomes. It protects the worker too — if something goes wrong and you followed policy, you have a defensible position.
"Now — professional boundaries. This is one of the trickiest areas in community services because good workers genuinely care about their clients. That caring is what makes the sector work. But caring can blur into over-involvement — and that's where both workers and clients get hurt."

Discussion question: "Can you think of a situation where a worker's genuine care might cross a professional boundary? What are the risks?"

Discussion prompt responses
Prompt responses if the group is quiet: Sharing personal phone number "just in case". Attending a client's family event. Lending money. Giving a client a lift outside of working hours. The risk in each: blurs the professional relationship, creates dependency, exposes both parties to potential harm, and may violate policy.
Experienced workers: ask them to share a situation they've navigated (without naming clients). Their real-world experience is a genuine teaching resource.
Newer students: the concept cards on the student page give a clear scaffold. Direct them to read Concept 3 (Professional Boundaries) before the discussion so they have language ready.
Concept 1 — AT1 KE1 + KE2.2
Professionalism & Policies
Acting within your role, following organisational policies, and upholding client dignity at every interaction. Policy = the 'what and why'. Procedure = the 'how'.
Key point for AT1: students should be able to explain why policies support ethical communication, not just that they exist.
Concept 2 — AT1 KE2.6 + KE2.7
Person-Centred, Rights-Based, Strengths-Based
Three lenses applied together — client goals first, rights are non-negotiable, focus on capacity not deficit.
Key point: students commonly write as if these are alternatives to choose from. Emphasise: you apply all three simultaneously.
Concept 3 — AT1 KE3.6 + PC1.3
Professional Boundaries
The limits defining what is appropriate within the worker's role. Protects both worker and client. Different from personal boundaries.
AT assessment link: AT2 Part B — students must write factual observations. Subjectivity = boundary failure of a different kind (values-based imposition).

🧭 Approaches: Person-Centred, Rights-Based, Strengths-Based, MI
Facilitation ScriptIntroducing the four approaches
▾
"There are four key approaches we use to guide how we communicate with clients. You've seen three of them in the concept cards. The fourth — motivational interviewing — is a specific communication technique worth knowing because it comes up in AT1 and in practice."

Direct students to the flip cards on their student page. Give 3 minutes for students to flip through independently, then debrief each one.

Debrief question for each card: "Can you give me an example of what this would look like — or sound like — with a real client?"

Model answers per approach
  • Person-centred: "I hear you want to stay in your home. Let's talk about what support would make that possible" (vs "You should consider residential care").
  • Rights-based: Ensuring a client with disability has the same access to information as any other client — large print, interpreter, extra time.
  • Strengths-based: "You've already navigated a lot to get here today. That takes courage. What else are you doing well?"
  • Motivational interviewing: "On a scale of 1–10, how ready do you feel to make this change? What would it take to move you one point higher?" — client identifies their own motivation.
Cue the Strengths-Based video now if timing works: youtube.com/watch?v=BALni0nEmGM. Even 3–4 minutes gives students a concrete example before break.
CALD students: their cultural frameworks often already use strengths-based and community-centred approaches. Acknowledge this as alignment with best practice, not a gap.

⚡ Conflict Management — Stages & Strategies
Facilitation ScriptOpening framing + stages walkthrough
▾
"Conflict is normal. It happens in every workplace, every family, every community. In community services, conflict can involve clients, families, colleagues, or organisations — and your response defines your professionalism."
"Let's look at how conflict develops — because the earlier you spot it, the easier it is to manage."

Walk through the stages on the student page with the group:

  • Latent — tension exists but hasn't surfaced. People might be avoiding each other, or working around an issue.
  • Perceived — one or both parties recognise there's a problem. They may not have named it yet.
  • Felt — the conflict is now emotional. People feel frustrated, hurt, or threatened.
  • Manifest — the conflict is visible. Arguments, avoidance, formal complaints, aggressive behaviour.
  • Aftermath — whatever remains after the conflict. This can be positive (resolution, learning) or negative (resentment, damage).
"The goal in community services is to catch conflict at Latent or Perceived — before it becomes Felt or Manifest. By the time it's Manifest, you're in crisis management mode."
Discussion question + model answer
Ask: "Think of a conflict you've witnessed or been part of — at work or in life. Which stage did it get addressed at? What would it have taken to address it earlier?"

Model: Latent conflicts often go unaddressed because naming them feels confrontational. The skill is learning to address tension early, before it becomes emotional. In community services this is often about having a regular check-in with clients and colleagues — "Is there anything that's not working for you?"
Facilitation ScriptFive strategies + de-escalation
▾
"There are five strategies for managing conflict. None of them is always right — except for one that's almost always worth aiming for. Let's go through them."
StrategyUse when…Risk if overused
AvoidingIssue is trivial or timing is wrongConflict grows underground
AccommodatingRelationship matters more than the outcomeWorker loses voice; resentment builds
CompromisingBoth parties need to give something quicklyNeither party fully satisfied
Collaborating ★Both parties work toward shared resolutionTime-intensive — but produces real outcomes
Competing ✗Rarely appropriate in community servicesDestroys relationships
"Now — de-escalation. This is one of the most practical skills you'll use. When a client is distressed or raising their voice, your instinct might be to match their energy or talk louder. Don't. Here's what actually works."

Walk through the de-escalation tips on the student page:

  • Lower your voice (don't match their volume)
  • Slow your pace — rushed responses escalate tension
  • Increase physical space — give them room
  • Open body language — uncrossed arms, slight lean
  • Name the emotion: "I can see this is really frustrating"
  • Avoid 'you' statements — use 'I' or neutral language
Practice prompt
"Turn to your partner. One of you is a client who is upset that their support hours have been cut. The other is the worker. Worker: practise de-escalating using the techniques above. 2 minutes — go."

Debrief: what felt natural? What felt awkward? Naming the emotion ("I can see this is frustrating") is often the hardest but most effective.
AT4 Part A link: "The conflict role play in Assessment Task 4 will test exactly these skills. Start thinking now about how you'd apply de-escalation in the scenario."
Newer students: the strategy table on the student page gives clear 'when to use' guidance. Refer them to it. Experienced workers: ask which strategies they've used successfully in real situations.

📋 Dispute Resolution
Facilitation ScriptDispute resolution vs conflict management
▾
"Conflict management and dispute resolution are related but different. Conflict management is what you do in the moment — strategies, de-escalation, communication. Dispute resolution is what kicks in when that doesn't work, or when the situation requires a formal process."
ProcessWhat it isWho's involved
NegotiationDirect discussion between parties to reach agreementThe parties themselves
MediationNeutral third party facilitates discussionParties + mediator
ArbitrationThird party makes a binding decisionParties + arbitrator
"In community services, you also have reporting obligations. If a conflict involves risk to a client or worker — or if it can't be resolved informally — you escalate. You don't handle it alone, and you don't keep it quiet. Know your organisation's escalation pathway."
Check understanding question
Ask: "A client's family member is angry about changes to the support plan and starts demanding to speak to someone senior. Which dispute resolution process is most likely next?"  — Answer: Mediation or escalation to supervisor/manager. Negotiation may have already been attempted by the worker.
AT1 link: students often mix up conflict management and dispute resolution in their answers. Make the distinction explicit: dispute resolution = formal, usually third-party involvement.

🃏 Activity: Scenario Cards
Activity GuideInstructions + debrief
▾
"I'm going to give each group a conflict scenario. Your task: identify which stage of conflict it's at, which strategy you'd use, and whether dispute resolution is needed. You have 5 minutes, then we'll share."

Suggested scenarios (print and cut into slips):

  • A client becomes increasingly withdrawn and stops engaging with their support worker. No complaint has been made. (Latent → Perceived)
  • Two colleagues are clearly avoiding each other after a disagreement about client care. Other staff have noticed. (Felt)
  • A client raises their voice at the front desk, demanding to see their case manager immediately. (Manifest)
  • A family member has lodged a formal complaint about the support their relative is receiving. (Dispute resolution territory)
  • A worker feels their manager consistently dismisses their ideas in team meetings. They've started staying quiet. (Latent → Felt)

Debrief: take one response per group. Ask: "What strategy did you choose? Why that one and not Competing?"

Groups of 3 work well for this activity. Pair quieter students with at least one experienced worker — the scenario discussion draws on real knowledge, which builds confidence.

👍 Boundaries or No Boundaries?
Activity GuideInstructions + statement-by-statement debrief
▾
"I'm going to read out 8 statements. For each one: thumbs up if it's appropriate professional practice, thumbs down if it's a boundary concern. No wrong answers at this stage — we'll discuss."

Give students a moment to respond, then take a few voices before revealing the intended answer.

  • 1. Worker shares their personal mobile number with a client "to stay in touch".
    Boundary concern
  • 2. Worker says "I understand that must be really hard" and checks in on the client's wellbeing.
    Appropriate
  • 3. Worker tells a friend about a client's situation because they're worried about them.
    Boundary concern
  • 4. Worker attends a client's birthday party at their home.
    Boundary concern
  • 5. Worker advocates strongly for a client's rights with their supervisor.
    Appropriate
  • 6. Worker cries during a client disclosure.
    Context-dependent — discuss
  • 7. Worker gives a client a lift home because they missed their bus.
    Boundary concern
  • 8. Worker uses the client's preferred name and pronoun consistently.
    Appropriate
Debrief ScriptHow to close the activity
▾
"What makes the line between caring and over-involvement so hard to hold in this sector?"

Take 3–4 responses. Likely answers: the work is emotional, clients can be vulnerable and develop strong attachments, workers want to help, policy feels cold when someone is in genuine need.

"That tension is real. It doesn't go away — you just get better at managing it. Having a supervisor you can talk to, knowing your policy, and building self-awareness are the main protective factors."

Note on Statement 6 (crying): This is genuinely context-dependent. Brief, authentic emotional response can be humanising. Extended emotional distress during a session is a problem — it shifts the focus from the client to the worker's needs. Discuss this with the group.

This is one of the most engaging activities of the session — give it the full time. It generates authentic discussion about real workplace dilemmas.

Facilitation ScriptHow to open this block
▾
"You've now got the content you need for AT1 Questions 7–12 and AT2 Parts A and B. This 75 minutes is your time to work on those. I'll be circulating — come and find me or raise your hand."
"One specific thing for those working on AT2 Part B — the incident report. The most common error I see is subjective language. I want you to think factual observations only. Not 'Cherie seemed angry'. That's your interpretation. What did you actually observe? 'Cherie raised her voice and stood up from her chair.' That's an observation. That's what goes in an incident report."
Common Error — AT2 Part B

Subjective: "Cherie seemed upset" / "Cherie appeared distressed" / "Cherie was aggressive"

Objective (correct): "Cherie raised her voice" / "Cherie stood up from her chair" / "Cherie said 'I don't want to talk about this'"

Incident reports are legal documents. What you write must be defensible. Model this language clearly before students start.
