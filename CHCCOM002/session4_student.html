Part 1 — Core Content
💡 Key Concepts
Concept 1
Technology in the Workplace
Digital tools in community services include email, Microsoft Teams / Zoom, and case management systems such as Brevity and CareVision. Privacy and confidentiality always apply — just because it's digital doesn't mean it's private.
"What you write digitally is permanent, can be forwarded, and may be read by supervisors, auditors, or legal teams."
Concept 2
Written Communication Principles
Clarity — say what you mean simply.
Accuracy — facts only, no opinions.
Brevity — as short as possible while complete.
Tone — match to your audience and purpose.
Audience Awareness — write for the reader, not yourself.
"Professional writing is not about big words — it's about being clear, accurate, and easy to understand."
Concept 3
Case Notes vs Incident Reports
Case notes = ongoing record of client interactions. Written after each significant interaction.

Incident reports = formal documentation of something that deviated from normal operations or posed a risk.

Both must be: factual, objective, no opinions, no assumptions.
"The test: could you defend everything you wrote in a legal proceeding? If not, rewrite it."

Part 3 — Documentation
📋 Case Notes vs Incident Reports

Both are legal documents. Both require factual, objective writing. The key difference is purpose and timing.

Feature Case Note Incident Report
Purpose Ongoing record of client interactions Formal record of an incident
When written After each significant interaction When something deviates from normal operations or poses a risk
Tone Factual, objective Factual, objective, chronological
Includes opinion? Never Never
Legal status? Yes Yes
The Legal Test

Ask yourself: could you defend every sentence you wrote in a legal proceeding? If the answer is no — or even maybe — rewrite it. Remove anything that is an opinion, assumption, or interpretation. Record only what you observed or what was directly stated.

Part 4 — Fix-It Activity
🔧 Fix the Documentation

Each example below contains a problem — opinion, assumption, vague language, or missing information. Rewrite it as a factual, professional statement in the text box. Your corrections are saved automatically.

Example 1
"Cherie seemed really angry and was being difficult."
'Seemed' and 'difficult' are opinions and judgements. Write what you observed: what did Cherie actually say or do?
Your correction
Example 2
"We had a great meeting today and everyone was happy."
'Great' and 'happy' are subjective — you cannot know how someone else feels. Record what happened and what was said, not your interpretation of the atmosphere.
Your correction
Example 3
"Client appeared upset (observation) due to family stress (assumption)."
Never mix observation and assumption in the same sentence. Record the observation only. If the client told you about family stress, record that as a client statement — not as a fact you are asserting.
Your correction
Example 4
"I think the client might be at risk."
'I think' and 'might' are vague and subjective. Be specific: what did you observe that indicated risk? Document the specific behaviour, statement, or situation — then report it through the correct channel.
Your correction
Example 5
"Spoke to John re: his situation. Will follow up."
Too vague — who is John (client/colleague/other)? What situation? What was the outcome of the conversation? What follow-up, and when? Documentation must answer: who, what, when, where, outcome, next action.

Key Terms
📖 Glossary
Case Note
A written record of a significant interaction with a client. Factual and objective — no opinions or assumptions. Completed after each relevant interaction.
Example: "Client attended appointment at 10:00am. Client stated they had not yet contacted their GP."
Incident Report
Formal documentation of any event that deviates from normal operations or poses a risk. Chronological, factual, and objective. Has legal standing.
Example: Used when a client behaves in a way that poses a safety risk, or when an accident or near-miss occurs.
Written Communication
Any message conveyed in written form — emails, texts, reports, case notes, letters. In community services, written communication is always professional and may be legally reviewed.
Style Guide
An organisation's rules about how written documents should look and sound — including formatting, tone, and required information fields.
Tone
The feeling or attitude conveyed in writing. In professional communication, tone should match the purpose and audience — formal for incident reports, direct but warm for client-facing letters.
Audience Awareness
Thinking about who will read your writing and adjusting language, detail, and format accordingly. Writing for a supervisor is different from writing for a client.
Confidentiality (Digital)
The obligation to protect client information in all digital formats — email, case management systems, messaging apps, and cloud storage. Digital information is permanent and can be forwarded.
Case Management System
Software used by community services organisations to record and track client information, case notes, incidents, and service delivery. Examples: Brevity, CareVision, Lumary.
Factual vs Subjective Writing
Factual writing records what can be observed, measured, or directly quoted. Subjective writing includes opinions, feelings, interpretations, or assumptions — these do not belong in professional documentation.
Factual: "Client raised their voice and stood up from their chair." Subjective: "Client was angry and difficult."
