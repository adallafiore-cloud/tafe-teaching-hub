🔄 The Communication Process
Content NotesKey concepts to cover + what to say
▾

Walk through Key Model Handout with the group. Cover in order:

  1. Interpersonal skills — definition and why they matter. Three core strategies: self-reflection, empathy, building diverse relationships.
  2. The communication cycle. Sender → Message → Channel → Receiver → Feedback.
  3. Noise and interference — anything that disrupts the cycle.
"Communication isn't one-directional. It's a cycle. When I send a message, I'm not done — I need to check it was received AND understood. That's where active listening comes in."

Learner Guide reference: Ch 1.1–1.2, pp. 5–11.

Noise and interference can disrupt any point in this cycle. Our job is to minimise it.
📋 Interpersonal Skills — 3 Key Strategies
  • Self-reflection — awareness of your own communication style and its impact on others
  • Empathy — understanding and sharing another person's perspective and emotional experience
  • Build diverse relationships — actively seek out and value connections with people from different backgrounds
Discussion PromptNoise — discussion prompt + model answers
▸
"What's an example of 'noise' that you've experienced in a community services context — something that got in the way of communication?"

Take 3–4 responses. Prompt if needed.

✅ Strong model answers
  • Physical noise — crowded waiting room, TV on in background, phone interruptions
  • Emotional noise — client is distressed, anxious, angry, or overwhelmed
  • Language barriers — different first languages, unfamiliar terminology, jargon
  • Power imbalance — client feels they can't speak freely or challenge the worker
  • Cognitive noise — worker is distracted, preoccupied, or making assumptions
Draw a simple cycle on the whiteboard and mark where noise can enter — students find this visual memorable for AT1.
Slower students: "Noise just means anything that gets in the way. What gets in the way when you're trying to talk to someone?" Keep it practical.

🤝 Non-Verbal, Listening & Communication Styles
Content NotesWhat to cover — in order
▾

Cover the following in order. This block covers Ch 1.3–1.7, pp. 12–30 of the Learner Guide.

  1. Non-verbal communication: body language, eye contact, facial expression, gestures, personal space, appearance
  2. Cultural influences on non-verbal communication — explicitly discuss with the cohort. Non-verbal norms vary (eye contact, silence, physical distance). This is high-value content for AT2.
  3. Active listening: paraphrasing, summarising, nodding, mirroring
  4. Questioning skills: open, closed, clarifying, probing — and when to use each
  5. Barriers to communication — full list (build from morning discussion)
  6. 7 communication styles — introduce before card sort activity
Communication styles vary across cultures — affirm CALD students' cultural communication knowledge as genuinely valuable, not as a deficit to overcome. "The way you communicate at home, in your first language, in your community — that is sophisticated knowledge. Bring it."

Non-Verbal Communication — key elements:

Body languageOpen posture signals safety; crossed arms can signal defensiveness

✅

Direct, honest, respectful — professional standard
⚠️
Aggressive
Forceful, disrespectful, intimidating — damages trust
⚠️

Indirect hostility — undermines relationships over time

Avoids conflict, but needs go unmet — can build resentment
🤝

Defers to others excessively — can compromise the work
🎭

Indirect, uses guilt or deception — breaks professional trust
📊

Logical, detail-focused — valuable but can feel cold if overused

Assertive communication is the professional standard in community services. Card sort activity (12:45pm) will reinforce this.

Barriers to Communication — Full List
  • Language and literacy differences
  • Cultural differences in communication norms
  • Physical environment (noise, privacy, comfort)
  • Emotional state (client or worker)
  • Power imbalance between worker and client
  • Assumptions and stereotyping
  • Physical or sensory disability
  • Technology limitations (poor phone connection, unfamiliar platforms)
  • Time pressure and workload
  • Lack of trust or rapport

🃏 Communication Styles Card Sort
Full InstructionsCard Sort — purpose, setup, facilitation, debrief
▾

Setup: 7 style cards + 7 scenario strips (prepared before class). Distribute one set per pair or small group.

In pairs: students match each scenario strip to the communication style they think it represents, then justify their answer to their partner.

"Match each scenario to the communication style. Then — tell your partner why you chose that one. What in the scenario gave it away?"

Allow 15 minutes for matching + pair discussion. Then debrief whole group (15 min).

Debrief questions — use these in order:

  • "Which styles are most common in community services work — from your experience?"
  • "Which styles are most damaging — to the worker, to the client, to the relationship?"
  • "What did you notice about the scenarios? Were any hard to identify? Why?"
✅ Model answers for debrief
  • Aggressive and passive-aggressive are most damaging — they erode trust and can cause client harm
  • Assertive is the professional standard — it balances honesty with respect
  • Passive can be hard to spot — it often looks like agreeableness but leads to unmet needs
  • Manipulative is most insidious — it's hard for clients to identify and respond to
Move around during the matching phase — give energy to quiet pairs. If a group finishes early: "What would the assertive version of this scenario look like?"
For students who are uncertain — prompt: "Which style makes the other person feel heard? Which one makes them feel threatened?" Often the emotional cue is more accessible than the label.
