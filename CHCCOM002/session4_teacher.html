New Learning 1
Technology in the Workplace
20 minutes

Cover digital tools used in community services, and establish the key principle: digital communication is permanent.

Key Points to Cover
  • Email, Microsoft Teams/Zoom — communication channels
  • Case management systems: Brevity, CareVision, Lumary — records management
  • Privacy and confidentiality apply regardless of the medium
  • Digital permanence: what you write can be forwarded, screenshots, audited, or subpoenaed
Discussion PromptActivate prior knowledge
▾
"Has anyone here worked in a place that uses a case management system? What did it look like — what could you see in someone else's notes?"

This surfaces the reality of shared record access. Experienced workers in the room may have strong examples — invite them to share briefly.

"Here's a question: if you sent a text message to a colleague about a client right now — who might be able to read that message? Think about your workplace phone policy."

Expected answers: supervisor, IT team, legal proceedings, Freedom of Information requests. Extend as needed.

For CALD students: digital communication norms and privacy law differ significantly across countries. Be explicit that Australian workplace law and the Privacy Act 1988 apply here — this is not assumed knowledge from home-country experience.
10:00 – 10:20
New Learning 2
Written Communication Principles
20 minutes

The five principles: Clarity, Accuracy, Brevity, Tone, Audience Awareness. Teach these as a framework students can apply to any piece of written communication — not just academic writing.

Facilitation ScriptTeaching the five principles
▾
"I want to start with something that surprises a lot of students. Professional writing is not about big vocabulary. It's about being clear, accurate, and easy to understand. If you use a complex word when a simple one works, you've made your reader's job harder — not easier."

Walk through each principle with a brief example:

  • Clarity — "The meeting will be at 10am on Thursday" vs "The meeting will occur at the abovementioned time." Which is clearer?
  • Accuracy — "Client stated she had not eaten today" (accurate) vs "Client seemed hungry" (interpretation).
  • Brevity — Say what you need to say. Stop. Don't add filler.
  • Tone — A formal incident report reads differently from a supportive email to a client. Both are professional. Different tone, same quality standards.
  • Audience Awareness — Write for the reader. A case note is read by your supervisor, a legal team, or an auditor — not by the client.
"So here is the single most important question to ask before you write anything: who is going to read this, and what do they need to understand from it?"
For CALD students: written professionalism norms vary across cultures. In some contexts, formal writing means very elaborate language — in Australian professional settings, the standard is the opposite: plain, direct, simple. This is not a lower standard. It's a different one. And it's one they can absolutely meet.
Allow verbal drafting before written — student talks through what they want to say, then writes. This is especially effective during the Fix-It activity later.

New Learning 3
Professional Emails — Structure and Tone
10 minutes
Email Structure to Cover
  • Subject line — specific, clear, professional. Not "FYI" or "Urgent!!!"
  • Greeting — Dear [Name] or Hi [Name] depending on relationship/context
  • Purpose statement — first sentence tells the reader exactly why you are writing
  • Body — factual, concise, no opinion
  • Close — Kind regards / Yours sincerely / Thanks
  • Signature — full name, role, organisation
Before / After DemoProject this on screen
▾

Project the Before & After comparison from the student HTML. Read both versions aloud. Ask:

"What is wrong with the first version? Give me three specific things — not just 'it's unprofessional.'"
Expected answers (model answers)
  • Abbreviation ('u') — not appropriate in professional communication
  • "Pretty upset" and "a bit messy" — subjective language, no factual detail
  • No clear subject or purpose — "Can we have a meeting or something?" is vague
  • No formal greeting or close

Then show the After version. Point out: same situation, same facts — completely different impression and professionalism.

New Learning 4
Case Notes — What They Are and How to Write Them
20 minutes
Facilitation ScriptWord-for-word teaching script
▾
"A case note is a running record of your interactions with a client. Every significant interaction should be documented. And when I say significant — I mean any interaction that might need to be referred to later."

Cover the required elements of a case note:

  • Who — client name, worker name, anyone else present
  • What — what happened, what was said (quote directly where possible)
  • When — date and time
  • Where — location or context
  • Outcome — what was agreed, decided, or noted
  • Follow-up — what happens next and who is responsible
"Now here is the critical rule — and this is the one most people get wrong. A case note must never contain your opinion about the client, your interpretation of why they did something, or your prediction about what will happen. Record ONLY what you observed or what was directly stated."
"The test I want you to use for every sentence you write: could I defend this in a courtroom? If the answer is 'I think so' — rewrite it. It needs to be an unambiguous yes."
For experienced workers: ask "Have you ever had to refer back to a case note that someone else wrote and found it was useless because it was vague or full of their opinions?" This almost always gets strong engagement.
11:05 – 11:25
New Learning 5
Incident Reports — When and How
20 minutes
Facilitation ScriptWord-for-word teaching script
▾
"An incident report is different from a case note. It's not a routine record of an interaction. It's a formal document triggered by something going wrong — or nearly going wrong."
"When do you write one? Whenever something deviates from normal operations. That could be a client becoming distressed in a way that was unsafe, a fall, a medication error, a situation where someone was at risk. You don't have to wait for an injury — if it was risky, it gets documented."

Cover the key structural requirements of an incident report:

  • Chronological order — what happened first, what happened next
  • Facts only — what you observed directly
  • No interpretation of why it happened
  • Client and worker details, date, time, location
  • Actions taken immediately
  • Persons notified
  • Outcome and follow-up
"Notice what's not in that list? 'What I think caused it.' 'What I think the client was feeling.' 'What I think should happen.' Your job in an incident report is to record what happened — not to analyse it."

Now present the Case Note vs Incident Report comparison table. Go through each row. Pause on the 'Includes opinion? Never' row — this always prompts questions.

Students often ask: "What if I was worried about the client — shouldn't I write that?" Answer: You document the observable indicators of risk — not your feeling of worry. "Client stated they had not eaten in two days" is better than "I was worried the client might be at risk of not eating."
11:25 – 12:00
Activity
Writing Workshop — Pairs Practice
35 minutes
Facilitation InstructionsWorkshop setup and facilitation
▾
"Right — let's put this into practice. In pairs, I want you to do two things. First, open the Email Builder in the student page and build an email from a community services worker to their supervisor requesting a meeting about a client incident. Second, write a four-sentence case note about a check-in appointment with a client who mentioned they hadn't been sleeping well."

Circulate during the activity. Watch for:

  • Vague subject lines in emails ("RE: client")
  • Opinion language in case notes ("seemed sad", "appeared stressed")
  • Missing elements — no date/time, no follow-up stated
  • Mixing observation with assumption in the same sentence

At 11:50: Bring the group together. Ask one pair to read out their case note. The group checks it against the 'legal test' — could every sentence be defended?

Model case note example

Date: 28 April 2026, 10:15am. Location: Client's home, 14 Smith St. Worker: [Name], Support Worker.
Client stated she had not slept well over the past three nights. Client stated she was unsure of the reason. No visible distress observed. Worker advised client to contact GP if sleeplessness continued beyond this week. Worker will follow up at next scheduled visit on 5 May 2026.

Warm-Up (Post-Lunch)
Fix-It Activity
30 minutes
Facilitation InstructionsRunning the Fix-It warm-up
▾
"Welcome back. We're going to shake off the lunch break with a quick Fix-It activity. I'm going to read five poorly written examples from documentation. Your job is to rewrite each one as a factual, professional statement. You have two minutes per example. Go."

Read each bad example aloud, clearly and without editorialising. Give students 2 minutes to rewrite. Then:

"Swap with your partner. Read their rewrite. Does it pass the legal test? Is there anything that could still be called an opinion or an assumption?"

Debrief question for the whole group:

"What was the most common type of error across all five examples?"
Expected debrief answer

The most common error is subjectivity — using opinion words ('angry', 'difficult', 'happy', 'worried') rather than observable facts. The second most common is vagueness — insufficient detail about who, what, when, and what happens next.

Students who have worked in the sector often have strong instincts here — they've seen bad documentation in practice. Let them lead the debrief. Your role is to confirm and extend, not to dominate.
For LLN students: allow verbal corrections first — "Tell me what you'd write, then I'll help you get it onto paper." This separates the thinking task (identifying the error) from the writing task (correcting it).
Fix-It Answers — Model Rewrites
  • 1. "During the 10:00am check-in appointment, Cherie raised her voice and stood up from her chair. Cherie then ended the appointment by leaving the room."
  • 2. "Team meeting held on 28 April 2026 at 10:00am. All team members attended. Agenda items were discussed and action items were allocated. [List actions if known.]"
  • 3. "Client's facial expression changed and client began to cry. Client stated, 'My family situation has been stressful lately.'"
  • 4. "Client stated they had not eaten since yesterday morning. Client stated they had no food at home. Worker contacted case manager [Name] at 11:20am to report. Worker noted this as a potential food security concern."
  • 5. "Worker spoke with client John [Surname], who is a NDIS participant, by phone on 28 April 2026 at 2:15pm. Client stated he was managing his daily tasks independently. Worker will follow up at next scheduled check-in on 5 May 2026."
