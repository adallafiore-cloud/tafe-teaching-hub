Part 1 — AT1 Key Content
💬 The Communication Process

Communication is not just talking. It is a two-way process — and when any part breaks down, the message is lost.

Concept 1
Interpersonal Skills
The skills needed to relate to and connect with other people. In community services, they help you build trust, understand client needs, and work effectively in teams.
Example: "Asking open questions to understand what a client actually needs."
Concept 2
The Communication Cycle
Sender → Message → Channel → Receiver → Feedback. Communication is a two-way process. When any part breaks down, the message is lost.
Example: "A worker explains a service plan clearly, checks the client understood, and adjusts based on their feedback."
Concept 3
Verbal vs Non-Verbal
Verbal = the words we choose, tone, pace, volume. Non-Verbal = body language, eye contact, facial expression, gestures, personal space. Research suggests non-verbal cues carry more meaning than words alone.
Example: "A client nods and says 'yes' but their crossed arms and avoiding eye contact suggest they're not comfortable."
Think about this

Think of a time when what someone said and what their body language said were different things. Which one did you believe? Why?

Part 3 — AT1 Key Content
🚧 Barriers to Communication

Even when we try to communicate well, things can get in the way. Recognising barriers is the first step to reducing them.

Things that get in the way
  • Language differences or limited English proficiency
  • Cultural differences in communication norms (e.g. eye contact, silence, directness)
  • Disability — hearing, vision, cognitive, or physical barriers
  • Emotional state — anxiety, grief, anger, or trauma can affect how someone receives a message
  • Environmental noise or a poor setting (lack of privacy, distractions)
  • Assumptions and unconscious bias — jumping to conclusions before listening
  • Power imbalance between worker and client — the client may not feel safe to speak up
Cultural note

What counts as 'normal' communication varies across cultures. Direct eye contact, silence, touch, personal space, and how disagreement is expressed all differ. There is no universal 'correct' way — your job is to tune in to the individual in front of you.

Reference
📖 Key Terms
Interpersonal skills
The skills used to interact and communicate with others. In community services, these include empathy, active listening, and the ability to build trust.
Communication process
The cycle by which a message moves from sender to receiver and back through feedback. All five parts — sender, message, channel, receiver, feedback — must work for communication to succeed.
Verbal communication
Communication using spoken or written words — including the words chosen, tone of voice, pace, and volume.
Non-verbal communication
Communication through body language, facial expressions, eye contact, gestures, posture, and use of personal space — often more powerful than words.
Active listening
Giving your full attention to a speaker — using nodding, eye contact, paraphrasing, and summarising to show you are engaged and to check understanding.
Paraphrasing: "So what I'm hearing is that you're feeling overwhelmed — is that right?"
Open question
A question that invites a detailed response — cannot be answered with just yes or no. Used to explore and understand.
"What has been the hardest part of this week for you?"
Closed question
A question with a short or yes/no answer. Used to confirm specific facts.
"Have you received your Centrelink payment this week?"
Clarifying question
A question used to confirm understanding and avoid assumptions.
"Just to check — you said you'd prefer morning appointments?"
Barrier to communication
Anything that prevents a message from being sent, received, or understood correctly — including language, emotion, environment, disability, or bias.
Assertive communication
Expressing your thoughts, feelings, and needs clearly and respectfully — without being passive (suppressing them) or aggressive (forcing them on others).
Community services professional standard: honest, direct, and kind.
